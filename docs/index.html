Can We Trust Marketplace Seller Ratings?

Two sellers can both average five stars. The evidence behind those averages can be very different.

By Daniel Zhao. I used SQL, Python and statistical modeling to test whether accounting for review volume improves predictions of later customer ratings.

Motivation

Same rating. Different evidence.

Previous work evaluating businesses and customer reviews inspired this independent study. When a marketplace gives two sellers the same average, how much confidence should a reader put in each score?

A small number of reviews can move an average substantially. Review counts help a reader see how much evidence each average carries.

Data and method

From order reviews to seller evidence

I used Olist’s Brazilian marketplace dataset. Its nine relational files cover orders, items, reviews, sellers, products, customers, geolocation, payments, and category translation.

A review describes an order experience. If multiple distinct sellers fulfil an order, the score cannot reliably be attributed to any one of them. I kept unambiguous single-seller orders with one review row, a unique review ID, a valid score, and a valid answer time. Repeated review rows and ambiguous IDs were excluded.

  1. Audit and linkInspect keys and join order, item, review, and seller records in DuckDB.
  2. Build the rating population95,245 eligible ratings across 3,064 distinct sellers.
  3. Split by score availability40,321 historical ratings across 1,741 sellers. 54,924 subsequent ratings.

Give sparse averages less weight

The raw estimate is a seller’s historical mean. The adjusted estimate blends it with the review-pooled historical average. The seller’s contribution grows with its review count. This is empirical-Bayes-style shrinkage.

r̂ = (n × r̄ + m × μ) / (n + m)

n = historical review count · r̄ = seller’s raw mean
m = shrinkage strength (20) · μ = pooled mean (4.153047 stars)

Historical evaluation

Only use scores that were available at the time

The historical period ends before January 1, 2018. Review answer timestamps are a proxy for when scores became available. The initial design split on survey creation, which could include scores answered after the cutoff in historical estimates. The corrected design uses answer time consistently.

Train, select, then evaluate

Before the cutoff. Two expanding chronological 2017 folds selected strength 20. The primary estimates then used all eligible historical answers.

After the cutoff. Compare both estimates with subsequent observed averages for the same 1,098 sellers. Each seller contributes one absolute error equally. MAE expresses the typical magnitude of those errors in stars.

Primary finding

Adjustment helped most when reviews were scarce

For sellers with 1–4 historical reviews, MAE fell from 1.062 to 0.721 stars, a 32.1% reduction. The gain was much smaller for sellers with 50 or more reviews.

Across the paired cohort · The absolute reduction was 0.155 stars, with a 95% paired seller-bootstrap interval of 0.124–0.186 stars. Resampling each seller’s two errors together preserves their pairing.

Ranking reliability

An exact rank can hide a tie

816 of the 1,098 paired sellers shared a raw rating with another seller. The largest tie covered 142 sellers. Mean absolute movement after adjustment was about 144 positions.

Accounting for review volume can materially change who makes a shortlist. The raw and adjusted Top 10 had zero sellers in common under every feasible ordering of raw-rating ties at the cutoff. The comparison measures how sensitive the shortlist is to the ranking method. Whether either list identifies better sellers remains an open question.

Spearman agreement was 0.828 and Kendall τ-b was 0.641.

Robustness and uncertainty

Prediction intervals missed their target

The bootstrap interval describes uncertainty in the aggregate MAE improvement. In the local report, model-conditional credible intervals describe uncertainty in a seller’s expected rating.

Predictive coverage asks how often intervals contained later observed seller averages, conditional on the realized subsequent review count. Reported coverage fell below 95%, especially in the 50+ historical review band.

Final-period sensitivity varied assumptions such as strength, cutoffs and minimum counts. The protocol identifies preplanned, supplemental and post hoc analyses. See robustness methods

Operational findings

Monitor fulfillment directly

Historical seller ratings had weak, noncausal associations with later lateness across 1,034 sellers. The adjusted-rating interval crosses zero.

Decision implications

Give the rating its context

  • Show review volume with seller averages.
  • Communicate rating uncertainty and explain ties.
  • Use direct measures for delivery performance.

Study limits

The findings come from one historical marketplace and a paired cohort with eligible ratings in both periods. Later observed averages are noisy measures of customer experience. Predictive intervals undercovered and operational associations were noncausal. Commercial impact was outside the evaluation.
